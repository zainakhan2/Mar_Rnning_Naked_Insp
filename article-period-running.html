Running on Your Period: What Nobody Tells You (But Should)

Let's talk about the thing that affects 50% of runners but appears in approximately 0% of running belt marketing: your period.

The Research: Period Running Is Not Only Fine — It Can Be Beneficial

A 2023 systematic review in the British Journal of Sports Medicine found that exercise during menstruation reduces cramp severity by 25-30%. Running specifically increases endorphin production, which directly counteracts prostaglandin-induced cramping. Translation: running on your period often makes you feel better, not worse.

But here's what the research doesn't address: the logistics.

The Logistics Nobody Discusses

Carrying Period Products on a Run

Where do you put a tampon or menstrual cup on a 10-mile training run? Most running gear has zero accommodation for this. Leggings have no pockets. Armbands hold a phone and nothing else. Even running belts with "multiple pockets" rarely consider that women might need a discreet compartment for period products.

This is why we built a hidden interior mesh pocket into the StrideHer valuables compartment. It's separate from your keys and cards, accessible without digging, and discreet. Because a $35 running belt shouldn't make you choose between carrying your phone and carrying a tampon.

What to Wear

Dark-colored running shorts or leggings. Period. (Pun intended.) Even with protection, anxiety about visible leaks is real and distracting. Black or dark navy removes that variable entirely. Some brands now make period-specific running shorts with built-in absorbent layers — worth investigating if you run high-flow days.

Hydration Adjustments

During your period, blood volume drops slightly and iron levels may decrease. You may feel thirstier than usual. Increase water intake by 250-500ml on run days during your period. Consider an electrolyte supplement if you experience cramping.

Training by Cycle Phase

Your menstrual cycle creates a 28-day hormonal rhythm that affects energy, recovery, and performance. Understanding this can help you train smarter:

Follicular Phase (Days 1-14): Build Phase

Estrogen is rising. Energy tends to be higher. Pain tolerance is elevated. This is when your body responds best to hard efforts — tempo runs, intervals, and long runs. Schedule your most demanding training sessions here.

Ovulation (Day 14): Peak Performance Window

Estrogen peaks. Many women report their strongest performances around ovulation. However, joint laxity also peaks (higher ACL injury risk) — warm up thoroughly and be mindful on trails.

Luteal Phase (Days 15-28): Recovery Phase

Progesterone rises. Core temperature increases slightly (you may feel warmer during runs). Perceived exertion may feel higher — the same pace feels harder. This isn't weakness; it's hormonal. Adjust expectations, focus on easy-pace mileage, and save the speed work for the follicular phase.

What Coaches Won't Tell You

Most running training plans are designed around a 7-day microcycle (one week). Your body operates on a 28-day cycle. The best approach: layer your training plan onto your menstrual cycle. Hard weeks during follicular phase. Recovery weeks during luteal phase. Race day during follicular or ovulation if possible (you can't always control race dates, but you can choose which races to target as A-races).

The Bottom Line

Running on your period is not only safe — it's often helpful for symptoms. The barriers are logistical, not medical. Carry what you need (in a belt that accommodates it), wear dark colors, hydrate more, and train with your cycle rather than against it. Your body isn't broken during your period. It's doing exactly what it's designed to do — and you can run right through it.
